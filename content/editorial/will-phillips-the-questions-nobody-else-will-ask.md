Will Phillips can tell you the exact moment his professional philosophy started, and he was eleven.

"When I was in the sixth grade, I remember thinking, man, what I'm doing isn't working. So I'm going to change myself so that I can better fit into my environment. And I kind of did and it kind of worked, but I was also mostly miserable."

It worked. That is the part that stays with me. The strategy succeeded and the cost was everything underneath it.

Most people carry a moment like that and never do anything with it. Will built a career on the conclusion he drew from his.

"I believe that the number one reason why people aren't thriving is because they're not in an environment that supports them to thrive."

He got a second education in the same lesson at his first real job, which was a high attention-to-detail role and a genuinely bad fit for someone with ADHD. He did not quit and he did not fail. He built systems to work around himself, and three years of that became a kind of masterclass in executive functioning.

He is refreshingly unsentimental about it.

"If I was designing the hiring system, I wouldn't have hired me, because I was a horrible fit for it. But what I did on the job is I figured out the systems I could develop so that I could operate out of my strengths and not my weaknesses."

That is a man who has thought seriously about the difference between a person being wrong for a job and a job being wrong for a person.

Will now works as a fractional people and culture executive, which in practice means he helps owners build the team they need so they can get out of daily operations. But the thing he says about the work that I have not heard anyone else say is about discovery.

He thinks his most valuable contribution is asking questions that make him look uninformed.

"A significant portion of the value I bring during the discovery stage is in asking stupid questions. Because the stupid questions, everyone knows the answer to them, and they're like, ooh, we don't talk about that thing."

Every organization has a set of facts that everyone knows and nobody says. They are usually the most important facts in the building. And the people who work there full time cannot raise them, because their mortgage and their sense of themselves are both attached to the answer.

Will can.

"Fractional people get to ask stupid questions because they're not anchoring their livelihood and their identity to this organization."

That is one of the clearest arguments for fractional leadership I have come across, and it has nothing to do with cost. It is about who is structurally free to say the true thing.

He is careful about it, though, which matters. Getting people to tell you the truth creates an obligation.

"Knowing what's appropriate to share and what could damage someone's career, it's our job to just get to the truth."

And he does not expect the truth on the first pass.

"Someone smart won't say the truth the first question. But then you ask the second question, you get a little bit more."

Underneath all of it is a conviction that the answers are already in the room. His Fortune 500 years taught him that teams usually know exactly what to do and cannot assemble it, because the knowledge is scattered across a dozen heads in fragments.

"Ninety-nine percent of the time, the business knows what it needs to do. And it's my job to just pull it out of people's heads and create alignment and momentum."

That is a rare posture for a consultant. It gives up the fantasy of arriving with the answer.

He is equally direct about the thing that quietly destroys companies, which is not a bad culture but a dishonest one.

"You say we're a family environment and then someone doesn't hit their numbers and they get fired. Well, no, really. That happens all the time because they're not a family environment."

The problem is not being a performance culture. The problem is being a performance culture that describes itself as a family, because then every consequence lands as a betrayal instead of a rule.

Will also has the single best piece of advice for a business owner I have collected in a long time, and it costs nothing.

"Give your best employee some responsibility that's on your plate right now. Don't tell them, do this task. Say, this is your responsibility. What can I do to help you be successful in this? I think you'll be blown away by what they can do for you."

Tasks make people faster. Responsibility makes them bigger.

He has a phrase for what fractional work feels like from the inside that I have not been able to shake.

"You get to enjoy it almost like an employee tourist. You're a part of the team without having to suffer the pain that people have to suffer as a result of certain cultural things."

Employee tourist. You are genuinely in it, you care about the outcome, and you also get to go home. That freedom is exactly what lets him ask the questions in the first place.

The sixth grader who changed himself to fit the room grew up to spend his career changing rooms to fit people.

That is not a tidy ending. It is just what happened.
